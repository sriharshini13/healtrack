Total Knee Replacement (Arthroplasty)

Procedure Overview

Replacing damaged knee joint surfaces with artificial components (implants) made of metal and plastic to relieve pain caused by arthritis and restore function.

Animation / Visual Guide

Surgeon's Explanation

"In this procedure, we carefully remove the worn cartilage and bone ends, precisely shaping the bone to fit the new metal and plastic components. This restores the joint's smooth surface."
- Dr. Emily Carter (Orthopedic Surgeon)

Key Information

  • Why it's done: Severe arthritis pain (osteoarthritis, rheumatoid), joint damage, significantly limited mobility unresponsive to conservative treatments.
  • Duration: Typically 1-2 hours surgery time.
  • Hospital Stay: Usually 1-3 days, depending on recovery progress.
  • Recovery: Requires dedicated physical therapy starting soon after surgery; significant recovery over 3-6 months, full recovery can take up to a year.
  • Risks: Include infection, blood clots, implant loosening or wear, nerve injury, stiffness. (Discuss all specifics with your surgeon).

Common Questions

Pain is expected initially but is actively managed with medication (oral, sometimes nerve blocks). Pain typically decreases significantly within weeks as you heal and participate in therapy.

Walking starts soon after surgery. Driving may resume in 4-6 weeks. Return to low-impact activities (swimming, cycling) often occurs within 2-3 months. Higher-impact activities depend on individual progress and surgeon's advice, typically 6+ months.

Appendectomy (Appendix Removal)

Procedure Overview

Surgical removal of the appendix, a small pouch attached to the large intestine. Usually performed urgently to treat appendicitis (inflammation/infection of the appendix).

Visual Guide (Laparoscopic)

Surgeon's Explanation

"We typically make 2-3 small incisions in the abdomen, insert a camera and specialized instruments, detach the appendix, and remove it through one of the incisions. It's generally a quick procedure."
- Dr. Kenji Tanaka (General Surgeon)

Key Information

  • Why it's done: Appendicitis, to prevent rupture and widespread infection (peritonitis).
  • Duration: Around 30-60 minutes for uncomplicated laparoscopic cases.
  • Hospital Stay: Often 1 day (or even same day discharge); longer if the appendix has ruptured.
  • Recovery: Gradual return to normal activities over 1-3 weeks. Avoid heavy lifting for several weeks.
  • Risks: Include wound infection, intra-abdominal abscess (collection of pus), bleeding. Open surgery may be needed if laparoscopic is not feasible or safe.

Common Questions

Appendicitis requires prompt medical evaluation and usually urgent surgery to prevent the appendix from rupturing, which is a more serious condition.

Usually, you can resume a normal diet gradually as tolerated after surgery. Start with clear liquids and advance slowly.

Cholecystectomy (Gallbladder Removal)

Procedure Overview

Surgical removal of the gallbladder, a small organ under the liver that stores bile. Most often performed due to gallstones causing pain (biliary colic) or inflammation (cholecystitis).

Visual Guide (Laparoscopic)

Surgeon's Explanation

"Through small abdominal incisions, we use a camera and tools to carefully dissect and remove the gallbladder. Bile will still flow directly from the liver to the intestine."
- Dr. Sarah Chen (General Surgeon)

Key Information

  • Why it's done: Symptomatic gallstones, gallbladder inflammation (cholecystitis), polyps, pancreatitis related to gallstones.
  • Duration: Typically 45-90 minutes.
  • Hospital Stay: Often performed as an outpatient (same-day) procedure or may require an overnight stay.
  • Recovery: Return to light activity within a few days to a week. Normal diet usually resumed quickly, though some may initially need to moderate fatty foods.
  • Risks: Include injury to the bile duct, bleeding, infection, hernia at incision sites, post-cholecystectomy syndrome (rare).

Common Questions

Yes, absolutely. The gallbladder primarily stores bile made by the liver. Without it, bile flows directly into the small intestine. Most people adapt well and don't notice significant long-term digestive changes.

During laparoscopy, CO2 gas is used to inflate the abdomen for better visualization. Some residual gas can irritate nerves causing shoulder or chest discomfort for a day or two. Walking helps dissipate the gas.

Inguinal Hernia Repair

Procedure Overview

Surgical correction of an inguinal hernia, where tissue (like part of the intestine) protrudes through a weak spot in the abdominal muscles in the groin area. Usually involves pushing the tissue back and reinforcing the weak area, often with surgical mesh.

Visual Guide (Laparoscopic)

Surgeon's Explanation

"Whether open or laparoscopic, the goal is to return the protruding tissue to the abdomen and strengthen the abdominal wall, typically using a mesh patch to prevent recurrence."
- Dr. David Miller (General Surgeon)

Key Information

  • Why it's done: Symptomatic hernia (pain, bulge), risk of strangulation (trapped tissue losing blood supply - an emergency).
  • Duration: Around 30-90 minutes, depending on technique and complexity.
  • Hospital Stay: Usually outpatient (same-day surgery).
  • Recovery: Return to light activities in 1-2 weeks. Avoid strenuous activity and heavy lifting for 4-6 weeks as advised by your surgeon.
  • Risks: Include pain (sometimes chronic), infection, bleeding, hernia recurrence, injury to nearby structures (nerves, blood vessels, vas deferens).

Common Questions

Follow your surgeon's specific instructions. Generally, avoid lifting anything over 10-15 lbs for the first few weeks, gradually increasing as healing progresses, typically resuming normal lifting after 4-6 weeks or longer.

Feeling Anxious? Let's Clarify Common Concerns

Myth: "Surgery is always extremely painful."
Fact: Modern anesthesia and pain management techniques significantly control discomfort. Our team works proactively to keep you comfortable during and after your procedure.

Myth: "I might wake up during the operation."
Fact: Anesthesia is administered and closely monitored by highly trained specialists (anesthesiologists or nurse anesthetists) throughout your surgery to ensure you remain safely asleep and unaware.

Myth: "Recovery means being stuck in bed for weeks."
Fact: For many surgeries, early mobilization (getting up and walking) is encouraged to speed recovery, improve circulation, and prevent complications like blood clots. Your care team will provide specific guidance.

Remember: Open communication with your surgeon and care team is vital. Write down your questions and bring them to your appointments. We are here to help you feel informed and confident about your care.
